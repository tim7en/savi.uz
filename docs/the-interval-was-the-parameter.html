The Interval Was the Parameter

The programme banked a 30-minute breakout book and spent eleven overlays trying to improve it. Swept across seven bar sizes at the cost this strategy actually pays, 30 minutes turns out to be close to the worst choice available — and a strategy nobody had tested beats all of them.

Seven intervals, and the banked one is a trough

Identical rules throughout — 55-bar Donchian entry, Wilder ATR(20), 2N stop, half-N pyramid to four units, 3N chandelier, six concurrent positions. Only the bar size changes. Both legs are stop orders, so 10bp taker is the cost that applies; 5bp is shown because every prior figure in the programme assumed something near it.

Why the trough is not noise

Cost is charged per trade regardless of how much a trade can earn, so slow channels keep more of what they make: 44,857 trades at five minutes against 9,563 at four hours. That accounts for the overall slope.

It does not account for 30 minutes sitting below both its neighbours. The 5–95% band there is [−0.02 to 0.32], which includes zero, so the honest reading is that the banked interval is indistinguishable from no edge at the cost it actually pays — while four hours reaches 0.95 with a band of [0.75–1.10].

The confirmation penalty is a fast-bar phenomenon

Waiting for a bar to close before entering costs a fixed 0.4N median of give-up per trade — scale-invariant, because N shrinks with the interval too. What changes is how often you pay it.

This overturns an earlier conclusion drawn from fast bars alone. Eight consecutive confirmation arms losing to a stop looked like a structural fact about confirmation; it was a fact about trade frequency. At daily bars the selection benefit of waiting for the close finally exceeds the price paid for it.

Buying the dip beats buying the breakout

A resting limit at trailing VWAP, taken only while price is above its long average. Filled at min(VWAP, open) when the bar actually trades down to it — a maker fill at 5bp, where the breakout's stop order is a taker at 10bp. Exits unchanged.

It clears the control that matters

Equities drift upward, so any long taken above a rising average earns money for free. The null draws the same number of entries from the same eligible bars with the same exits. The dip rule beat it in 15 of 15 draws, 1.30 against 0.79.

The null still captures 61% of the result — in line with the 63–75% that random entries captured from the breakout book. Most of what both strategies earn is drift. The dip rule simply keeps more of it.

Two details that decide it

The regime filter is load-bearing. 1.30 with it, 0.85 without. Dip-buying with no trend condition really does catch falling knives, and the difference is 0.45 Sharpe.

Accelerating on depth adds nothing. 1.32 at 5bp and 1.24 at 7.5bp, against 1.30 and 1.28 flat. Sizing up into deeper dips feels right and measures as noise.

Not yet bankable. No out-of-sample split, no per-year table, and the 10bp arm is still computing — and the dip book takes five times as many trades as the breakout book, which is exactly the exposure that cost has punished everywhere else in this study. interval sweep running

Crossovers and price-versus-average are not the same test. Price-versus-average asks where price is; a crossover asks whether the fast mean is rising through the slow one. The first survives its reversal at every window tried. All three crossovers lose to theirs, and 50:200 loses worst — halving size when the fast average is below the slow one actively helps, which is the opposite of the proposal.

What follows

  1. Re-base the programme on 4-hour bars. The banked 30-minute configuration scores 0.20 at its true cost with a band including zero. Every overlay in the ledger was tested against that configuration.
  2. Run T1.1 out-of-sample on the 4-hour book, parameters frozen first. It has never been run on anything, and nothing above is bankable without it.
  3. Finish the dip study. The 10bp arm and the interval sweep decide whether it is a general effect or a slow-bar, maker-only one.
  4. Quote costs by execution type, not by preference. A stop order is a taker. A resting limit is a maker. The dip strategy's advantage is partly that its entry is structurally cheaper, and that is a real edge rather than an accounting choice.
