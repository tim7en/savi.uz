The Filing and the Slot

A 13F cannot tell you when to buy — that is measured, not asserted. What it can do is name companies somebody researched, and change the shape of what happens after those companies break out. Two chapters, three claims, and three very different grades of evidence behind them.

The load-bearing caveat

Chapter six earned its result with a properly constructed null: hold the calendar and the counts fixed, redraw which tickers were accumulated, 2,000 times. The observed tail gap sat above the ninety-eighth percentile. It is the first thing in the programme to clear a real null with room to spare.

Chapter seven runs no null at all. Its headline — capacity priority lifting full-period Calmar from 0.583 to 0.712 — comes from substituting roughly fourteen net trades out of 694 and gaining 147R. A large return change from a handful of substitutions is precisely the high-variance situation a null exists to test. The result may well be real; as it stands it is untested, and it should not be graded alongside chapter six's.

What the filing cannot buy

The disclosure lag is not a friction on the edge. It is larger than the edge.

Measured, not argued

New positions entered at the quarter-end the filing describes — which no outsider can trade — return +1.58% over one quarter. The same positions entered the month after the filing became public return −3.39% over four quarters and −5.67% over eight.

The gap between the two runs 2.4 to 4.1 percentage points, and it is the whole of the effect. Forty-five days is the difference between a manager's return and a follower's.

Two self-corrections worth keeping

Sorting new positions by market capitalisation today produced a beautiful monotone ladder: under $300m returned −22.6% with t = −4.14, rising cleanly to +6.9% above $50bn. It is an artefact. Today's market cap is a post-outcome variable, so the small buckets fill with losers by construction. Reconstructed as at the filing, the ladder disappears entirely.

And one filer, Baron, supplies 757 of 1,252 new positions. Removing it moves the headline from significantly negative to indistinguishable from zero. A 400-name book and an 11-name book should never have been pooled.

What it can buy: the shape, not the centre

A trend follower cuts one tail and keeps the other, so the mean is the wrong statistic. Here the mean says nothing and the tail says everything.

Why the mean misses it

Measured as excess over the average breakout that month, the difference in means carries a t-statistic of −0.57 — nothing, and negative at that, because a few catastrophic left-tail outcomes dominate the average. An analyst reading only mean excess return would report no effect and move on.

The effect is entirely in the shape, and shape is exactly what a stop-loss-and-pyramid system converts into money. This is the one place in either chapter where the finding and the machinery genuinely fit each other.

Chapter seven, and where it stops being evidence

Chapter six ends by naming its own limit: the breakout there is a monthly twelve-month high, and whether the tail effect survives at daily 55/20 is untested. Chapter seven is that test — but it changes the question along the way.

The hard gate is a different strategy

Taking only active high-conviction names cut full-period CAGR from 22.70% to 9.15%, and Calmar from 0.583 to 0.238. It looks strong in the 2023+ slice — Calmar 1.864 against 1.151 — but that is one favourable sub-period against a halved full-period result. The chapter is right to reject it as a quality filter.

The risk tilt is not exposure-matched

The multiplier is bounded below at 1.0 and rises to the cap, so a tilted book always carries more risk than the baseline. Mean multiplier was 1.087× and the drawdown duly rose from −38.92% to −41.26%.

The programme's own first standing control is matched drawdown. Comparing a 1.087× book against a 1.000× book at fixed risk-per-R is the exact comparison that rule exists to forbid. The honest test is to scale the baseline up by 1.087 and re-run; that was not done.

What would settle chapter seven. Three things, none expensive. A null that shuffles conviction weights across tickers while holding the calendar and counts fixed, exactly as chapter six did — if random labels reproduce a +0.13 Calmar gain often, the result is noise. A per-year table, because the standing contract requires seven years in ten and the chapter reports only two aggregate windows. And a matched-exposure re-run of the tilt. Until then the priority rule is a candidate, not a finding.

What is licensed today

  1. Do not copy the filings. Established, with a measured mechanism: the forty-five-day lag exceeds the edge.
  2. Use disclosed conviction as a watchlist. Admit newly disclosed positions at or above 5% of a concentrated manager's book, keep them eligible for one year, and let the existing price rules decide entry, size and exit. This rests on the tail result, which survived a real null.
  3. Treat slot priority as untested. It is cheap and it is plausible, but it has faced no null and no per-year test. Running the chapter-six null against it is the single highest-value hour available on this thread.
  4. Freeze the risk multiplier. Chapter seven says this itself, and the exposure-matching flaw makes it firmer: the 2× cap is evidence to test forward, never permission to change live risk.
