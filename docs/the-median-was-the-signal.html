Margin of safety · 118 US names · 2010–2025 · SEC company facts, 90-day reporting lag

The Median Was the Signal

Nine tests of a single idea: buy good businesses when they fall. Price drawdown alone failed, and failed backwards — deeper was worse. Quality screens did not rescue it. Then the horizon changed from two months to three years and the same screens started working, for a reason the averages had been hiding the whole time.

Drawdown alone fails, and it fails backwards

The first version was the plain idea: buy a discount to the rolling high. The prediction registered before running it was monotonicity — deeper discount, better outcome. That is a claim that can fail cleanly, and it did.

A shallow pullback beat the baseline; a real drawdown collapsed to nothing. And the sweep agreed without being asked — handed depths from 10% to 30%, every one of the top six in-sample configurations chose 10%, the shallowest available. Out of sample the whole thing scored 2.64 against a drift null of 2.38, p = 0.37, and buying new highs instead scored 2.32. Three ways of saying the same thing: the discount was not the signal.

The screens did not rescue it

The obvious fix is that a discount is only safe if the business behind it is. Survival, quality and valuation gates were built from the SEC company-facts store and put in front of the same trigger.

Every interval overlaps every other one

The screens scored 2.11 and their exact inverse scored 2.59, which is tempting to read as “junk wins”. It is not readable at all: the gap is 0.48 and one standard error is 0.6. All four arms contain the null. Worse, the 658 trades sit in only 112 distinct months and cluster into a handful of regimes, so the true error is larger still.

What the screens were really selecting

Matched 368 pairs on month, drawdown depth, beta and volatility, so fundamentals were the only thing left differing. At 60 sessions the gap vanished — +0.26%, t = 0.26. The accounting screen had been a convexity proxy wearing an accounting costume.

What actually moves a two-month rebound

With the screen question settled, the useful thing was to ask what does predict a rebound. A regression on 1,817 crossings, standardised so magnitudes compare, with market state and stock convexity fitted alongside the accounting variables.

Market fear is the strongest variable in the study, and it is a timing input rather than a screening one — you are paid for supplying liquidity when liquidity is scarce. Volatility, beta and leverage carry the stock-level signal; leverage at 60 days is the Merton effect showing up directly, since a levered firm’s equity is a call option on its assets and rebounds multiplicatively. Valuation and profitability say nothing, and the drawdown depth says nothing at all.

One caution about my own output: fitting valuation against VIX produced coefficients of +271.8 and −271.8 on the main and interaction terms. That is a collinearity blow-up, not a finding, and the regression cannot answer whether fundamentals are priced differently in calm markets. The tercile split could, and showed no advantage to the screens in any regime.

The horizon was the mistake

Everything above measures a rebound over twenty to sixty sessions. That is a real finding about rebounds and a worthless one about investing, because nobody buying a good business at a discount expects to be judged in two months. Changing the horizon to three years, and the benchmark to an equal-weight hold of the same names in the same month, changes the answer.

Why the median and not the mean

On an earlier, narrower cut the lowest-quality decile showed a mean excess of +58.9% and a median of −47.4%. Both are correct. The typical low-quality business loses roughly half its value against its peers, and the positive average is carried entirely by a few multi-baggers buried in the bucket.

That distinction decides which statistic to use. A strategy holding nine hundred names collects the mean. A strategy holding fifteen collects something much closer to the median. Mean reversion is paid by the tail; compounding is paid by the median — and quality only looks good under the second, which is the correct one for owning businesses.

And then the drawdown earns its place

The last row is the value trap in one number. The same drawdown that helps a good business by 9.6 points hurts a bad one by 44.4. The discount is not the signal; the discount is a multiplier on whatever the business already was.

Two things wrong with the quality score

It disqualifies banks rather than judging them

Citigroup, Bank of America and Baker Hughes sit in the top quintile 0% of the time, because solvency is scored as one minus liabilities over assets and that is near zero for any bank by construction. The score is not measuring their quality, it is excluding their business model. Real sector-specific metrics are required and the store does not carry them.

Its winners are this era’s winners

The names most often ranked highest are GOOGL 100%, MSFT 97%, AAPL 87%, UNP 84%, SPGI 83%, META 73%. A quality score that ranks mega-cap technology top through 2010–2025 will look prescient whether or not it measures quality, and this data cannot separate those two possibilities.

Nor is the score the one the framework wanted. The SEC company-facts store carries ten concepts. Free cash flow, EBITDA, net debt, interest coverage and analyst revisions are all absent — so a net-debt/EBITDA threshold cannot be tested, ROIC becomes ROE, and a discount to appraised fair value becomes a cross-sectional book-yield rank. What is measured here is a reduced proxy, and the reduction is severe enough to name.

What this adds up to

  1. A falling price is not a margin of safety. Measured three separate ways: deeper was worse, the sweep chose the shallowest discount offered, and drawdown depth carried a t-statistic of 0.36 in the regression.
  2. Over two months, rebounds are convexity and market fear. Volatility, beta and leverage predict; valuation and profitability do not. VIX at entry is the strongest variable anywhere in the study.
  3. Over three years, quality orders the median and the win rate. Only at the extremes of the ranking — the middle six deciles carry no information.
  4. The two combine, but only deep. Top-quality bought more than 30% down is the one positive median and the one win rate above 50%. A 15% discount adds nothing.
  5. The same discount destroys a bad business. Bottom quality bought down 15%+ returns −44.4%.

The specification that survives is narrower than the one it started from: rank on quality, take the top decile rather than the top half, wait for a drawdown beyond 30% rather than beyond 10%, and hold for three years. It is also the only result in this research that grew stronger when a benchmark error was corrected rather than collapsing.

What happened when it was traded

This study measured episodes. The obvious next question is what a book running these rules returns, and that was built afterwards on 134 companies from October 2010 to August 2026 — entries sized by risk, held to the prior high or three years, financing charged. It changes two of the conclusions above and leaves the central one standing, weakened.

Quality holds up, more weakly than this reads

At matched gross exposure the quality gate returned +5.8% a year against a null median of +4.5% — the 88th percentile of 25 random draws of the same size from the same drawdown pool. Same direction as the three-year result here. Not significant: one draw in eight beat it.

Cheapness selects backwards

An earnings-yield cut above the universe median landed at the 28th percentile of its own null — worse than choosing at random from the same drawdowns, and the expensive half returned more. This study found valuation inert at two months. Traded over three years it is not inert, it is mildly harmful.

And the drawdown depth did not reproduce — at the depth tested

Running identical gates at a 5% pullback returned +4.9% against the 18% version’s +5.0%. On index funds depth is the whole finding; on single companies, at these depths, it is worth nothing.

This is not a refutation of the section above. The cell that survived here was the top quality decile bought more than 30% down — and the portfolio test used a median-rank quality cut at 18%, so it never ran that cell. What it does establish is that the depth effect is not general: it does not appear at 18% on a median-quality screen, so whatever is happening at 30% in the top decile is narrower than a reader of this page would assume, and rests on the smallest sample here.

The portfolio results, both universes and the full set of controls are in Eighteen Percent. The headline there is that no arm beat holding the market: equal-weight buy-and-hold of the same 134 names returned +18.1% a year, DIA +10.6%, and the best gated arm +6.7%.

118 US-listed names on split-adjusted daily bars, ranked monthly from October 2010 to August 2025; 13,866 name-months, of which 11,233 support a three-year forward window. Quality is a monthly cross-sectional rank of return on equity, operating margin, one minus liabilities over assets, and negative share-count growth, gated on positive net income; at least three of the four components must be present. Company facts come from SEC XBRL filings and are held unreadable for 90 days after the period they describe, because the store records a period end and no filing date. Excess return is measured against an equal-weight hold of exactly the names ranked that month, so the full cross-section sums to zero and what remains is selection rather than survivorship. Medians are reported ahead of means throughout because the excess distribution is strongly right-skewed and every mean here is carried by a small number of extreme winners. Three-year windows over fifteen years give roughly five independent periods, and the short-horizon tests draw on events that cluster into far fewer distinct months than their trade counts suggest.
