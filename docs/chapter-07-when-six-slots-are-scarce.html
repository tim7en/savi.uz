When Six Slots Are Scarce

A public filing cannot say when to buy. It can say which breakout deserves a scarce place in a full book, and how much risk the book can justify taking.

I · The question changes with capacity

The filing is not an entry.

The original 13F result was clear: copying a manager after a forty-five-day disclosure lag is not a trade. The unresolved question was narrower: when a six-position Turtle book has more valid breakouts than room, can disclosed conviction decide which one gets the slot?

The test takes every long-only 55-day channel breakout across the 143-name panel. Entry remains a stop at the channel edge; the system retains its 20-day exit, 2N protective stop, half-N pyramiding to four units and 2bp round-trip cost. A name becomes conviction-active only after its actual public 13F filing, where a concentrated manager reported a new position of at least 5% of the disclosed book. It stays active for one year.

There are two distinct uses of that information. A hard gate takes only active names. Priority leaves every valid breakout eligible, but gives an active high-conviction name first claim on a same-day slot. The second is an allocation rule; the first is a different strategy.

II · What the allocator sees

A hard gate is a trade-off. Priority is the cleaner result.

The hard gate greatly reduced drawdown in the 2023+ validation slice, but it also cut the full-period CAGR from 22.70% to 9.15%. It is therefore not a free quality filter. Capacity priority uses the same full opportunity set, changes no stop or exit, and only breaks same-day contention using information public before the entry.

The result that survives both periods: priority raises the full-period Calmar ratio from 0.583 to 0.712, while validation rises from 1.151 to 1.521. It does this with fewer selected trades and lower exit-marked drawdown, rather than by simply increasing aggregate leverage.
III · Conviction and risk

More size helps here. That does not make it a rule yet.

The sizing test leaves every breakout in the book. It applies a multiplier that rises linearly from 1.0 at zero disclosed conviction to the cap at 10% of a manager's book. The largest tested cap, 2x, did not double portfolio exposure: qualifying positions were uncommon, so the mean risk multiplier was only 1.087x over the full period and 1.088x in validation.

That cap improved return and Calmar in both samples, but the drawdown rose from -38.92% to -41.26% over the full period. It was tested alongside two smaller caps after the original 13F result was known. The right conclusion is not “double risk”; it is that the multiplier is a candidate for a frozen forward test.

IV · What is licensed

A forward rule, not a retrospective promise.

  1. Track actual public 13F filings. Admit only newly disclosed positions at or above 5% of a concentrated manager's reported equity book.
  2. Keep each name on the watchlist for one year after the filing. Do not buy it because of the filing.
  3. Use the existing long-only daily 55/20 Turtle entry, stop, pyramid and exit rules unchanged.
  4. When same-day entries compete for the sixth slot, rank active names by their largest public manager weight before using a random tie break.
  5. Freeze any risk multiplier before forward deployment. The 2x cap is evidence to test, not permission to change live risk.
Three limits travel with this result. The 143-name panel is the union of historically observed high-conviction positions, so its absolute return inherits hindsight-universe bias. Alpha Vantage daily history is a current vendor history, not an archived vintage. And the drawdowns here are marked only when a trade exits; intratrade adverse excursion would be worse. The priority comparison is cleaner because it holds the panel, entries, exits and capacity fixed, but it is still a research result rather than a live mandate.
