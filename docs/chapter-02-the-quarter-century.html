Chapter Two

The Quarter Century

An investor who has not troubled to learn what the market did to people before him is in no position to judge what it may do to him. The ground beneath the argument, before any strategy is discussed

What a hundred dollars became

The first duty of a market history is arithmetic, and the arithmetic of the eleven American sectors since January 2000 is less dramatic than the period's reputation suggests. A hundred dollars committed to consumer discretionary shares at the opening of the century, with dividends reinvested, became $1,068. The same hundred in financial shares became $521. Between the best sector and the worst lies a factor of two — over twenty-six years that encompassed two catastrophes and the longest bull market in living memory. The investor who agonised over sector selection was arguing about less than he supposed.

The ranking itself would have surprised the participants. Technology, whose collapse opens the period and whose ascent closes it, finishes fourth. Energy, which spent the decade after 2014 being written off, finishes third. Utilities — the dullest holding available, the one an ambitious investor would have been embarrassed to name — finish sixth, ahead of materials, staples and financials. Only real estate and communications trail badly, and both are shortened histories rather than poor ones: neither fund existed for the whole period.

The dividend, which is most of the answer

The gap between the two bars in that chart is the part of the return that arrives as cash rather than as price, and for several sectors it is larger than the price gain itself. Utilities returned $321 on price alone and $811 with dividends reinvested — the income more than doubled the outcome. Financials show the same pattern, $251 against $521. Technology, which pays little, shows the smallest gap of the long-lived sectors at 35%.

This is not a footnote to be acknowledged and set aside. An investor comparing sectors on price charts — which is what most charts are — would conclude that utilities returned a third of what discretionary shares did. With dividends counted, the ratio is closer to three-quarters. The entire case for one sector over another can be manufactured or destroyed by the choice of measure, and the measure is usually chosen by whoever drew the chart rather than by the investor reading it.

The index and its twin

The same distinction governs the market as a whole, and here it is worth $336 on a hundred. The S&P 500 price index turned $100 into $535 over the period, a compound 6.5% a year. Its total-return twin turned the same $100 into $871, or 8.5%. Two percentage points a year, compounded across twenty-six years, is the difference between tripling one's money and multiplying it by nine.

The two lines below are the same market. They diverge for no reason other than bookkeeping. An investor who spent the period reading the first line and receiving the second was consistently more prosperous than he believed — and one who compared his own dividend-paying holdings against the price index was flattering himself by exactly the amount of his own income.

On the two catastrophes. The price index fell 57% between October 2007 and March 2009, and 49% between March 2000 and October 2002. The Nasdaq 100, over the same span, fell 83%. An investor holding that index needed a six-fold gain merely to return to where he began, and did not see it again until 2015 — fifteen years of waiting to break even, during which the arithmetic of the thing was never in doubt and the patience of the holder was tested continuously.

Commodities, duration, credit and the dollar

Outside equities the period was far less kind, and in two instruments it was ruinous in a way that no market decline explains. Gold turned $100 into $933 since November 2004, compounding at 10.8% — better than the S&P's total return over a comparable window, and the single best holding in this table. Investment-grade credit returned 4.4% a year, long Treasuries 3.6%, the dollar 1.6%.

Then the wreckage. A hundred dollars in the crude oil fund became $24; in the natural gas fund it became, to the nearest dollar, nothing. The volatility fund lost 51.8% a year, every year, for seventeen years. These are not bets that went wrong; they are instruments that consume capital by construction. Each holds futures that must be rolled forward, and each rolled at a loss so persistently that the underlying commodity's own price movement became irrelevant. Natural gas cost roughly the same in 2026 as in 2007. The fund that tracked it lost essentially everything.

How the sectors answer to interest rates

Beneath the sector returns lies the most dependable relationship in this entire record, and it is not a market opinion but very nearly an accounting identity. When the ten-year Treasury yield rises by a percentage point, real estate shares fall 4.4% relative to the market, and utilities 3.8%. Financial shares rise 3.3%, energy 2.8%. Nine of the eleven sectors show the effect at a statistical strength that leaves no room for argument — utilities at a t-statistic of 20, real estate at 17, financials at 16.

The mechanism is a discount rate, and it requires no cleverness to see. A utility is a long stream of regulated cash flows; a real estate trust is a long stream of rents. Both are, in the arithmetic that matters, bonds wearing equity certificates, and both must fall when the rate at which their distant cash is discounted goes up. A bank, by contrast, earns the spread between what it pays for deposits and what it charges for loans, and a steeper curve widens that spread directly. These are not correlations discovered by searching; they are consequences one could have written down in advance — and, unusually for this book, they are.

A caution the investor should take from this section rather than a recommendation. The relationship is real, large and stable. It does not follow that it can be turned into money. A later chapter puts precisely this measurement to work — sizing positions by it, gating their direction, switching sectors off when the rate environment is against them — and reports four separate failures, each against a control designed in advance. The reader is asked to hold both facts at once, because the distance between them is the subject of this book.

The sectors through the cycles

Set the same eleven sectors against the eight episodes that make up the period and a second pattern appears, older and simpler than the rate arithmetic: the holdings that protect capital in the declines are the ones that surrender it in the advances. Consumer staples were the best sector of the dot-com decline, losing nothing across two and a half years while technology lost 82%. They were the best again through the financial crisis, down 29% against financials' 81%, and best again in the pandemic crash. In every one of the three great advances they finished last or nearly so.

This is the whole of defensive investing in one row of a table. The staples holder was not clever; he was differently exposed, and he paid for his comfort in the years when comfort was unnecessary. An investor who wants the protection must accept the cost, and the cost is paid continuously while the protection is drawn upon rarely. Energy demonstrates the reverse with equal clarity: worst in the pandemic crash at −56%, best in the recovery that followed at +166%, best again in the tightening bear of 2022 when everything else fell.

What the market was willing to pay

Valuation over the period did not oscillate around a mean so much as visit two extremes and spend most of its time nearer the higher one. On a cyclically adjusted basis — price against a ten-year average of earnings — the market began the century at 47.9, a level without precedent in the record and not approached since. It fell to 24.5 by the 2002 trough, recovered to 30.7 before the 2007 peak, and reached 14.9 in March 2009. That last figure is the only genuinely cheap reading in twenty-six years.

Since then the direction has been one way. The pandemic low of 26.7 was higher than the 2007 peak. By December 2021 the ratio stood at 42.9, and it has spent the years since between 40 and 43 — a range that in 1999 would have been considered a bubble and is now simply the weather. The investor who resolved in 2013 to wait for cheapness has now waited thirteen years, and the discipline that protected him in 2000 has cost him the entire subsequent advance.

The economy underneath

The real economy grew steadily and unspectacularly throughout, which is worth stating because the market's behaviour bore so little resemblance to it. Real output rose 75% across the period. Consumer prices rose 97%, so that a dollar of January 2000 purchases 51 cents today. Unemployment began at 4.0% and stands at 4.1% — a symmetry that conceals a journey to 14.8% in April 2020 and down to 3.4% in April 2023, the lowest reading in half a century.

Set the two records side by side and the disproportion is the lesson. Output grew by three-quarters; the total-return index multiplied by nearly nine. The excess is not production. It is some combination of profit margins, the price the market is willing to pay for a dollar of earnings, and the long decline in interest rates that ended in 2022. Only the first of those is properly the investor's, and none of the three is his to forecast.

The rate cycles, and the four inversions

The clearest macroeconomic signal available to an investor over this period announced itself four times, and the record of what followed is neither as reliable nor as useless as either camp maintains. The ten-year Treasury yield fell below the two-year in March 2000, again in December 2006, and most recently between July 2022 and August 2024 — the longest inversion ever recorded, twenty-five months. The first two preceded the two great declines of the era. The third preceded nothing: equities rose strongly throughout it.

That is the whole sample. Three episodes, two of them followed by disaster and one by a boom, and the investor is invited to draw conclusions. He should decline. A signal with three observations is not a signal; it is an anecdote with a chart. The deepest inversion of the period, at 1.04 percentage points in July 2023, was followed by two of the better years in the record.

What the period offers, and what it withholds. Twenty-six years supplies a great deal of data and remarkably few independent events. There are two equity catastrophes, one pandemic, one inflation, three inversions and perhaps six distinct rate cycles. Every claim in the chapters that follow — about regimes, about positioning, about which conditions favour which holdings — rests on a denominator of that size, whatever the number of daily observations may suggest.

This is the reason the arithmetic came first. An investor who knows that a hundred dollars became somewhere between five hundred and eleven hundred almost regardless of where he put it, and that the difference between the best sector and the worst was smaller than the difference between counting dividends and ignoring them, is properly armed against the chapters that follow. He will not expect too much from them.
