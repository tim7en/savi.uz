Chapter Two

The Quarter Century

An investor who has not troubled to learn what the market did to people before him is in no position to judge what it may do to him. The ground beneath the argument, before any strategy is discussed

The century and a half before this one

Before examining twenty-six years closely it is worth establishing whether they were ordinary, and the only way to know that is to look at a great deal more of them. A dollar committed to American equities in January 1871, with dividends reinvested and the result measured in constant purchasing power, became $32,759 by September 2024. That is 7.0% a year after inflation, sustained across 154 years, two world wars, the Depression, and the transformation of the currency — prices rose twenty-five-fold over the same span, so a nominal figure would have described the dollar rather than the market.

Seven per cent real is the number an investor should carry in his head. It is the reward for owning productive assets over a period long enough that no individual's fortune, folly or timing matters. Almost every claim made about markets is a claim about deviations from it.

The deviations are large and last longer than a working life is patient. Real returns by decade have ranged from −3.1% a year in the 2000s to +16.6% in the 1920s. Three decades of the fifteen delivered nothing at all: the 1910s, the 1970s and the 2000s each left a buy-and-hold investor poorer in real terms after ten years of waiting.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it frames everything that follows. The quarter century this chapter examines contains the worst decade in the entire record and the fifth best, back to back. An investor who drew conclusions from either half alone would have drawn opposite ones, and both would have been wrong.

The record also contains fifteen occasions on which American equities lost more than a fifth of their real value, and the worst of them took away three-quarters. The 1929 peak was regained, in real terms and with dividends reinvested, in November 1936 — seven years, not the twenty-five usually quoted. That familiar figure describes the price index alone, and the gap between the two is the same dividend that this chapter keeps returning to: reinvesting income into a collapsed market does more work than any other decision available to an investor. The decline that began in August 2000 ran eight and a half years and cost 52% — the second worst in the series, and one this chapter's period contains in full.

And on valuation, the present is not ordinary. Over 154 years the cyclically adjusted ratio has had a median of 16.5. A quarter of the time it sat below 12.0, and only one month in twenty saw it above 31.4. The most recent reading is 35.2 — the 97th percentile of the entire record.

This is not a forecast, and the reader should resist converting it into one; the ratio has been in its top decile for most of the last decade while the market rose. It is a statement about the price being paid, and therefore about the margin of safety available, which is a different thing from a statement about what happens next. The long rate, over the same span, has ranged from 0.62% to 15.32%.

What a hundred dollars became

The first duty of a market history is arithmetic, and the arithmetic of the eleven American sectors since January 2000 is less dramatic than the period's reputation suggests. A hundred dollars committed to consumer discretionary shares at the opening of the century, with dividends reinvested, became $1,068. The same hundred in financial shares became $521. Between the best sector and the worst lies a factor of two — over twenty-six years that encompassed two catastrophes and the longest bull market in living memory. The investor who agonised over sector selection was arguing about less than he supposed.

The ranking itself would have surprised the participants. Technology, whose collapse opens the period and whose ascent closes it, finishes fourth. Energy, which spent the decade after 2014 being written off, finishes third. Utilities — the dullest holding available, the one an ambitious investor would have been embarrassed to name — finish sixth, ahead of materials, staples and financials. Only real estate and communications trail badly, and both are shortened histories rather than poor ones: neither fund existed for the whole period.

The dividend, which is most of the answer

The gap between the two bars in that chart is the part of the return that arrives as cash rather than as price, and for several sectors it is larger than the price gain itself. Utilities returned $321 on price alone and $811 with dividends reinvested — the income more than doubled the outcome. Financials show the same pattern, $251 against $521. Technology, which pays little, shows the smallest gap of the long-lived sectors at 35%.

This is not a footnote to be acknowledged and set aside. An investor comparing sectors on price charts — which is what most charts are — would conclude that utilities returned a third of what discretionary shares did. With dividends counted, the ratio is closer to three-quarters. The entire case for one sector over another can be manufactured or destroyed by the choice of measure, and the measure is usually chosen by whoever drew the chart rather than by the investor reading it.

The index and its twin

The same distinction governs the market as a whole, and here it is worth $336 on a hundred. The S&P 500 price index turned $100 into $535 over the period, a compound 6.5% a year. Its total-return twin turned the same $100 into $871, or 8.5%. Two percentage points a year, compounded across twenty-six years, is the difference between multiplying one's money by 5.4 and multiplying it by 8.7.

The two lines below are the same market. They diverge for no reason other than bookkeeping. An investor who spent the period reading the first line and receiving the second was consistently more prosperous than he believed — and one who compared his own dividend-paying holdings against the price index was flattering himself by exactly the amount of his own income.

On the two catastrophes. The price index fell 57% between October 2007 and March 2009, and 49% between March 2000 and October 2002. The Nasdaq 100, over the same span, fell 83%. An investor holding that index needed a six-fold gain merely to return to where he began, and did not see it again until 2015 — fifteen years of waiting to break even, during which the arithmetic of the thing was never in doubt and the patience of the holder was tested continuously.

Commodities, duration, credit and the dollar

Outside equities the period was far less kind, and in two instruments it was ruinous in a way that no market decline explains. Gold turned $100 into $933 since November 2004, compounding at 10.8% — better than the S&P's total return over a comparable window, and the single best holding in this table. Investment-grade credit returned 4.4% a year, long Treasuries 3.6%, the dollar 1.6%.

Then the wreckage. A hundred dollars in the crude oil fund became $24; in the natural gas fund it became, to the nearest dollar, nothing. The volatility fund lost 51.8% a year, every year, for seventeen years. These are not bets that went wrong; they are instruments that consume capital by construction. Each holds futures that must be rolled forward, and each rolled at a loss so persistently that the underlying commodity's own price movement became irrelevant. Natural gas itself fell by about half, averaging $6.97 in 2007 against $3.63 in 2026 — a decline no holder would welcome, and nothing remotely like the fund's outcome. The commodity halved; the instrument tracking it lost essentially everything.

How the sectors answer to interest rates

Beneath the sector returns lies the sharpest relationship in this record, and also the most easily misread: it describes what happens on the day rates move, and says nothing whatever about the day after. On sessions when the ten-year Treasury yield rises by a percentage point, real estate shares fall 4.9% relative to the other sectors, and utilities 4.2%. Financial shares rise 3.7%, energy 3.1%. Nine of the eleven sectors show the effect with t-statistics between 5 and 13, computed with standard errors that allow for the autocorrelation daily returns carry.

The mechanism is a discount rate, and it requires no cleverness to see. A utility is a long stream of regulated cash flows; a real estate trust is a long stream of rents. Both are, in the arithmetic that matters, bonds wearing equity certificates, and both must fall when the rate at which their distant cash is discounted goes up. A bank, by contrast, earns the spread between what it pays for deposits and what it charges for loans, and is helped when long-term rates rise. The measurement here is against the ten-year yield alone, so it establishes the direction of the effect without isolating the slope of the curve as its cause; that remains an explanation rather than a finding. These are not correlations discovered by searching; they are consequences one could have written down in advance — and, unusually for this book, they are.

What they are not is a forecast. Lag the yield change by a single session — ask not how sectors move with rates but how they move after them — and the structure evaporates. Financials fall from +3.68 to +0.13, technology from +1.49 to +0.04, utilities from −4.22 to −0.51. The shrinkage is not uniform — materials keeps 36% of its coefficient and industrials 18% — but it is sharpest exactly where the same-day effect was largest, and what survives is marginal: the strongest residual carries a t-statistic of 2.0 against 13 for the contemporaneous version. This is repricing, not prediction. Sectors do not respond to yesterday's rate move; they are marked to today's as it happens, and by the close there is nothing left to collect.

A caution the investor should take from this section rather than a recommendation. The same-day relationship is real and large. It cannot be used to forecast direction, and the reason sits in the table rather than in a later chapter: acting on it would require knowing the day's yield move before the day's close, and by the close the repricing is done.

Sizing is a different question with a different answer. The dates on which rates are most likely to move — policy meetings, inflation and payroll releases — are published months ahead, and an investor who merely carries less on those days uses no forecast at all. That is a risk decision rather than a return one, and a later chapter treats it as such.

A later chapter puts the measurement to work anyway — sizing positions by it, gating direction, switching sectors off when rates are against them — and reports four failures, each against a control fixed in advance. Those failures look less like bad luck than like the arithmetic of the next-day column being what it is.

What actually moves rates when the Fed speaks

The preceding section measured how sectors respond to a change in the ten-year yield, without asking what caused the change. Narrow the question to the minutes around a Federal Reserve announcement, and a result emerges that overturns most of what is said about these occasions. Across 212 scheduled meetings since 2000, the surprise in the rate the committee actually set explains half of one per cent of the move in the ten-year yield. Its correlation with that move is 0.07.

Add what the meeting implied about the next meeting and the figure rises to 11.6%. Add the full year of expected policy embedded in futures and it reaches 42.5%. The decision itself is very nearly irrelevant; what matters is the revision to the path.

This deserves dwelling on, because it explains why so much commentary about these occasions is beside the point. Markets do not move on the decision; they move on the revision to everything expected after it. A quarter-point rise that everybody anticipated is worth nothing whatever. The same rise accompanied by projections implying two more is worth a great deal.

Measured inside that window the sector responses are larger and cleaner than the all-day figures suggest. A ten-basis-point upward repricing moves financials 0.60% against the other sectors and real estate −0.66% — a spread of more than a percentage point, opening in half an hour.

A correction to the previous section, arrived at by narrowing the question. The all-day regression does not measure the market's response to the Federal Reserve. It measures the response to the ten-year yield moving for any reason at all — inflation figures, employment reports, Treasury auctions, foreign selling, a flight to safety. When the cause is isolated to a policy announcement, technology's apparent sensitivity disappears entirely and health care changes sign.

Financials, utilities and real estate survive the narrowing intact, and are the only three an investor should treat as established.

What the committee was looking at

An obvious objection to everything above is that the Federal Reserve is not capricious. It responds to inflation and employment, both of which are published in advance, so if the reaction is known the outcome ought to be knowable too. The objection is sound and the answer to it is the heart of the matter. What the committee does is largely predictable. What the market learns is the gap between that and what it had already priced, and a gap between two forecasts is not itself forecastable.

Taking the conditions prevailing before each of 212 meetings — monthly figures only where they had certainly been published, market prices from the previous session — and sorting the meetings by what the funds rate did over the following four months produces a portrait of the reaction function.

The tightening column is unsurprising: inflation running at 2.77%, payrolls adding 341,000 a month, credit spreads at their narrowest. The holding column is the zero-bound era wearing a disguise — unemployment averaging 6.54%, inflation below target, and a balance sheet growing at 22.8% a year, which is what quantitative easing looks like when reduced to a single number.

The cutting column contains the surprise, and it is worth an investor's attention. Unemployment before a cut averaged 4.56% — fractionally higher than before a rise, and nowhere near distress. What distinguishes the two is the labour market's momentum rather than its level: payrolls were adding 43,000 a month before cuts against 341,000 before rises, an eightfold difference in the flow with almost no difference in the stock.

The committee does not wait for unemployment to climb. It moves when hiring decelerates, which happens first. An investor watching the unemployment rate for a signal about policy is watching the slowest instrument on the dashboard, and will read it after the decision has been taken.

And so to the objection. Fitting these six conditions on meetings before 2017 and applying them to the seventy-six since, the direction of the next move in the funds rate is called correctly 68% of the time against 54% for the trivial rule of always guessing the commoner outcome. That is real, if modest, foresight about what the committee will do.

The same six conditions, applied to what the ten-year yield does in the announcement window, are correct 47% of the time against a 53% baseline — worse than guessing. The economy tells you what the Fed will decide. It tells you nothing about what you will learn when it does.

One honest caveat on both figures: measured by squared error rather than direction, each model fails badly, because 2022 lay outside anything in the training period and no linear rule extrapolates to it. Even where the direction of policy is foreseeable, its magnitude in an unfamiliar regime is not — which is a second reason, independent of the first, to distrust anyone forecasting these occasions.

What an investor can do about it

Two questions must be kept apart, because the evidence answers them very differently: whether one can know which way rates will move, and whether one can know when they are likely to move a great deal. The first is hopeless. The second is published on a calendar a year in advance.

On direction the record is unambiguous. A model given every scrap of information available before a meeting — the shape of the forward curve, the gap between the current rate and where the market expects it to be, recent movement in those expectations, the level of volatility, whether projections were due — and trained on seventeen years of meetings, achieved an out-of-sample R² of −0.70 across the following seventy-six. Its directional accuracy was about 47%, against roughly 53% for the trivial rule of always guessing the commoner direction. It was worse than useless.

What makes this decisive rather than merely disappointing is the comparison with perfect foresight. A position holding financials against utilities through the announcement, placed by an oracle who knew which way the yield would go, would have earned about eight basis points a meeting after costs. The same position placed by the model lost about seven. The relationship is easily strong enough to profit from; the direction of the surprise is simply not knowable beforehand, which is what the word surprise means.

Risk is another matter entirely. Whether a meeting carries updated economic projections is settled when the year's calendar is published. Those meetings move the ten-year yield an average of 3.90 basis points against 2.28 for the others, and produce a move of five basis points or more 37.8% of the time against 10.3%. In data the rule never saw, that difference survives at p = 0.004.

The defensible course, then, is to size rather than to steer. An investor holding rate-sensitive positions — utilities, property, banks — may reasonably carry fewer of them into a meeting that will publish projections, and restore the position afterwards. This requires no view on what the committee will decide. It requires the calendar, which costs nothing.

Three cautions attach, and they matter more than the finding does. Reducing exposure reduces return as well as risk, and the investor must satisfy himself he is not merely holding a smaller portfolio while paying to trade it. What is measured here is a narrowing of the worst outcomes, not an improvement in the average one. And the same arithmetic governs any hedge: an offsetting position carried through every meeting pays its spread every time and earns its keep only in the few that surprise.

An investor who wants protection on these dates should be clear that he is buying insurance rather than finding an edge. That is a respectable purchase at a fair price and an expensive habit at any other.

Having recommended it, we tested it, and it fails — for a reason worth more than the recommendation was. Applying exactly this rule to a diversified momentum book of forty-two American shares, reducing exposure on every scheduled meeting and restoring it the next session at a round-trip cost of ten basis points, made matters steadily worse the more it was applied.

The table falls away on both sides of the middle, which is the shape of a genuine optimum rather than of noise, and the middle is doing nothing at all. Every variant but one is also worse than simply holding a book of the same average size every day, so it is the timing that costs rather than the amount.

The randomised control confirms the calendar is not meaningless, and in the process explains itself. Against two hundred calendars of randomly chosen days, cutting on the real Federal Reserve dates ranks at the ninety-sixth percentile from the wrong end, while adding on them ranks better than the random median. The sign is right in both directions. These are identifiably good days; the trouble is that there are seventy-six of them in two and a half thousand.

The reason is that these are the best days of the year to be invested. The book earns +11.9 units of risk on a meeting day against +5.4 on every other day, and finishes higher 56.6% of the time against 49.9%. Dispersion is barely wider — 36.4 against 33.5. The investor is being paid roughly twice the usual rate for slightly more than the usual risk.

This is the announcement premium that researchers have documented for years, arriving in a place we did not think to look for it. The event risk is real, and it is compensated. Selling it because it is measurable is how an investor converts a premium he was collecting into a cost he now pays.

Nor can the premium be seized by leaning into it. Doubling exposure on the same dates also underperforms doing nothing, and the reason is arithmetic rather than behavioural. Those seventy-six days contribute about 6.5% of the book's total result. Doubling them adds 6.5% to the return, but variance grows with the square of the weight, so holding to the same drawdown forces the base position down by eleven per cent — across the other 2,415 days, which is where the money is actually compounded. The net is 1.065 against 0.889, and it is negative.

Three per cent of the calendar cannot carry a portfolio, however good those three per cent are. The one condition under which the reasoning does work is an investor already carrying less risk than he is willing to bear: for him the extra exposure is free and the premium accrues. The loss here comes entirely from the assumption that he is already fully invested.

Which leaves the chapter's lesson intact and pointed at neither extreme. Knowing when risk is elevated is genuinely useful; assuming it should be avoided is not, and assuming it should be chased is not either. The distinction between insurance worth buying and a premium worth collecting is the whole of what this chapter has to teach — and the arithmetic here says to collect it by standing still.

A note on method, since it cuts against the result as much as for it: every variant is scored at the risk level that produces an eighteen per cent drawdown across the whole period, which no investor could have known in advance. It is applied identically to each, so the comparison is fair, but a live book could not calibrate itself so precisely. The dates themselves carry no such problem — the Federal Reserve publishes them about a year ahead.

The sectors through the cycles

Set the same eleven sectors against the eight episodes that make up the period and a second pattern appears, older and simpler than the rate arithmetic: the holdings that protect capital in the declines are the ones that surrender it in the advances. Consumer staples were the best sector of the dot-com decline, losing nothing across two and a half years while technology lost 82%. They were the best again through the financial crisis, down 29% against financials' 81%, and best again in the pandemic crash. In the three great advances they finished last or nearly so.

This is the whole of defensive investing in one row of a table. The staples holder was not clever; he was differently exposed, and he paid for his comfort in the years when comfort was unnecessary. An investor who wants the protection must accept the cost, and the cost is paid continuously while the protection is drawn upon rarely. Energy demonstrates the reverse with equal clarity: worst in the pandemic crash at −56%, best in the recovery that followed at +166%, best again in the tightening bear of 2022 when everything else fell.

The policy regimes, and what led in each

Rates do not merely change; they sit in states, and the states last for years. Classifying every day since 1999 by where the funds rate stood against twelve months earlier produces nine spells of six months or more: three tightenings, three easings, two long stretches at the floor, and one period on hold. The floor alone accounts for ten years of the twenty-six — ninety-seven months after 2008 and twenty-six more after March 2020.

This is the regime structure an investor actually lived through, and it is coarser than the commentary suggests. There have been three tightening cycles in twenty-six years. Any claim about how sectors behave when the Fed raises rates rests on three observations.

A regime is labelled by the change in the effective funds rate over the preceding twelve months: a half point or more in either direction, or below half a per cent for the floor. Sector returns are total return, and a sector is shown only where its fund covered at least ninety per cent of the spell. The thresholds are chosen with hindsight and describe the record rather than a rule anyone could have applied on the first day of a regime.

The table's most useful feature is its inconsistency. Technology was the worst sector of the 2001 easing, losing 46%, and the best of both subsequent tightenings, gaining 43% and 45%. Energy was the best sector of a tightening cycle and also the best of a period pinned at the floor, two conditions with nothing in common. No sector leads two regimes of the same kind and no sector trails two.

Set against the earlier finding this makes sense. The rate sensitivities measured in that section were same-day repricings of a few tenths of a per cent. A tightening cycle lasts two years and contains an economy, an earnings season, a technology cycle and whatever else occurs. The discount-rate effect is real and is swamped over any horizon an investor actually holds for.

Not even the strongest case survives the table. Real estate carries the largest negative rate coefficient of any sector, at −4.89 per percentage point, and it duly finished worst of the 2022–24 tightening, falling 12% while the market rose. But it lived through two tightenings, and in the other — 2017 to 2019 — it gained 27% and energy finished worst instead.

So the sector with the cleanest measured sensitivity to rates got the direction right in one of the two cycles available to test it. That is what a real mechanism looks like when it is swamped by everything else: present in the arithmetic, unreliable in the outcome. The reader should be suspicious of anyone building a rotation on it — the sample that would support the claim is small enough to print in full, and has been, immediately above.

What the market was willing to pay

Valuation over the period did not oscillate around a mean so much as visit two extremes and spend most of its time nearer the higher one. On a cyclically adjusted basis — real price against a ten-year mean of real earnings — the market began the century at 43.2, a level without precedent in the record and not approached since. It fell to 22.0 by the 2002 trough, recovered to 27.3 before the 2007 peak, and reached 13.3 in March 2009. That last figure is the only genuinely cheap reading in the period.

Since then the direction has been one way. The pandemic low of 24.8 sat below the 2007 peak of 27.3 and comfortably inside the 13.3-to-32.1 range the ratio had covered between 2002 and 2017 — a fall, but not to anything a long-run investor would call cheap. By December 2021 the ratio stood at 38.3, and it has since ranged between 27 and 39 — a band that in 1999 would have been called a bubble and is now simply the weather. The investor who resolved in 2013 to wait for cheapness has now waited more than a decade, and the discipline that protected him in 2000 has cost him the entire subsequent advance.

The economy underneath

The real economy grew steadily and unspectacularly throughout, which is worth stating because the market's behaviour bore so little resemblance to it. Real output rose 75% across the period. Consumer prices rose 97%, so that a dollar of January 2000 purchases 51 cents today. Unemployment began at 4.0% and stands at 4.1% — a symmetry that conceals a journey to 14.8% in April 2020 and down to 3.4% in April 2023, the lowest reading in half a century.

Set the two records side by side and the disproportion is the lesson. Output grew by three-quarters; the total-return index multiplied by nearly nine. The excess is not production. It is some combination of profit margins, the price the market is willing to pay for a dollar of earnings, and the long decline in interest rates that ended in 2022. Only the first of those is properly the investor's, and none of the three is his to forecast.

The rate cycles, and the four inversions

The clearest macroeconomic signal available to an investor over this period announced itself four times in three clusters, and the record of what followed is neither as reliable nor as useless as either camp maintains. The ten-year Treasury yield fell below the two-year twice during 2000, once from October 2006 to March 2007, and most recently between July 2022 and September 2024 — the longest episode in this sample at twenty-six months, though the record here begins only in 2000. The first two clusters preceded the two great declines of the era. The third preceded nothing: equities rose strongly throughout it.

That is the whole sample. Three clusters, two of them followed by disaster and one by a boom, and the investor is invited to draw conclusions. He should decline. A signal with three observations is not a signal; it is an anecdote with a chart. The deepest inversion of the period, at 1.04 percentage points in July 2023, was followed by two of the better years in the record. Note too that both the merging of brief interruptions and the thirty-day minimum are applied looking backwards: on the first inverted day an investor cannot know which sort of episode he is in.

What the period offers, and what it withholds. Twenty-six years supplies a great deal of data and remarkably few independent events. There are two equity catastrophes, one pandemic, one inflation, three inversions and perhaps six distinct rate cycles. Every claim in the chapters that follow — about regimes, about positioning, about which conditions favour which holdings — rests on a denominator of that size, whatever the number of daily observations may suggest.

This is the reason the arithmetic came first. An investor who knows that a hundred dollars became somewhere between five hundred and eleven hundred almost regardless of where he put it, and that the difference between the best sector and the worst was smaller than the difference between counting dividends and ignoring them, is properly armed against the chapters that follow. He will not expect too much from them.
