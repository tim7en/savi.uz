Where the Bar Closes

A breakout that spikes through the channel and closes near the low of its own bar is not a weak signal. It is a losing one — it stops out 86.8% of the time. This is the largest conditional effect measured anywhere in the programme. It is also untradeable — acting on it costs more than it pays.

The catch: none of it is knowable when the order fills

An arithmetic problem, not a statistical one

A stop order fills mid-bar at the channel edge. The bar's close, its high and its volume are all final only when the bar is. Acting on any of these three features therefore requires close-confirmed entry, which surrenders the channel fill.

That cost is measured: at 5bp, plain stop entry scores 0.59 against close-confirm's 0.22 — about 0.37 Sharpe given up to make the bar observable. So the question is whether a +2.343R selection effect is worth more than the entry it costs.

The threshold response is non-monotonic — −0.09, +0.06, −0.03, −0.03 — peaking at 0.62 and falling away at tighter cutoffs. That is the same inverted-U that condemned theme trend strength, so even the +0.06 is the best cell of a small sweep rather than a signal. And the entry costs more than flat slippage: close-confirm produced 70,261 trades against stop entry's 51,759, because paying up at the next open puts the fixed 2N stop proportionally tighter and you stop out more often.

The number the whole programme turns on

A stop order is a taker: when a buy-stop triggers it becomes a market order. The chandelier exit is also a stop. This strategy is taker on both legs, so 10bp is its real cost, not 5bp.

At 10bp, plain stop entry scores 0.19, with a 5–95% band of [−0.02 to 0.32] across capacity orderings. The band includes zero. Every other figure on this page — including the moving-average rule's 0.84 — is quoted at 5bp maker, which this entry mechanism cannot obtain.

Why this argues against buying the pullback

Targeting a better fill by waiting for price to return toward VWAP looks like free money and is the opposite. A limit order below the breakout only fills when price comes back — and the breakouts that come back are by construction the ones closing lower in their range.

That is quintile one and two: mean R −1.203 and +0.226, stopping out 86.8% and 76.3% of the time. You would be systematically filled on the losers and missed on the winners, buying a better price on precisely the trades not worth owning.

Any test of a pullback entry has to count unfilled signals as forgone winners rather than dropping them. Otherwise it reports an excellent average fill price and a worse book.

The moving-average result is the real one

It is the first overlay in the programme to beat its own reversal consistently across a parameter sweep. Halving size below the average helps; halving above it hurts by a similar margin, so the label carries directional information rather than merely modulating exposure.

It also explains why the earlier higher-timeframe trend filter was rejected. That was a gate, and removing those trades removed +1,205R of winners along with the losers. Halving keeps the winners at reduced size. Gate versus sizing is the whole difference.

Two ways to be fooled, both avoided here

Exposure is not a strategy. Constant 70%, 85% and 100% produce identical Sharpe once matched to the same drawdown — only the risk-per-R differs. Any rule that merely changes average size is re-parameterising leverage, which is why the reserve rule contributed +0.009.

Random tie-breaks hide everything. The Sharpe band across capacity orderings is [0.57–0.96]. Effects smaller than 0.4 are invisible unless the comparison is paired on the same orderings, which every comparison above is.

What still has to happen

  1. Re-price everything at 10bp. Both legs are stop orders and therefore taker fills. Plain stop entry scores 0.19 there with a band spanning zero, so every 5bp figure in the programme — including the moving-average rule — needs restating before any of it is bankable.
  2. Per-year consistency on the moving-average rule. The standing contract requires seven years in ten and only the aggregate has been measured.
  3. Out-of-sample validation with parameters frozen first. This is T1.1, it has never been run on anything, and every result here is scored against a configuration that has not passed it.
  4. Correlation-grouped capacity. With 72% of breakouts refused, the tie-break governs most trading decisions. Grouping needs no forecast to justify itself, unlike ranking — and any deterministic rule cuts the Sharpe band roughly fourfold whether or not it raises the median.
