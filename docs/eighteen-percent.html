Eighteen Percent

A discount below eighteen percent buys nothing: the same forward return, the same downside, just later. Above it the arithmetic changes. That is true of index funds. Carried to a hundred and thirty-four individual companies it stops being true — the depth threshold does nothing, and the gate meant to keep you out of expensive quality turns out to have been keeping you out of the better half. And for a levered book the whole framing is wrong: what stops leverage paying is not the borrowing cost, which four entries in five already beat, but being closed out before the recovery arrives.

The threshold, and why it is the whole rule

Margin of safety is measurable: what the three-year gain is worth against the further fall you have to survive to collect it. Both halves were measured on every entry, and only one of them moves with depth.

The further fall is constant at 15–17% however deep you buy. That is the fact the whole rule rests on: the risk does not grow with the discount, so every point of extra depth is free margin. It also means depth buys return, not leverage capacity — the tail stays the same size, so the leverage ceiling never moves.

The tail is real and no entry depth removes it

One entry in twenty goes 60% against you before it recovers; one in a hundred goes 70% against. At 3× leverage that is −180% and −209% — a total loss, twice over. This is on equity index funds, not single stocks, and after excluding futures-backed and volatility products that decay for unrelated reasons.

Which fixes the sizing: a position that survives its own fifth percentile is risk ÷ 60% of the book. At 1% risk that is 1.7% per position. Not intuition — arithmetic.

It does not beat holding the index

+2.5% against +6.2%, 1.95× money against 5.02× over twenty-seven years. The low drawdown is not risk management, it is absence: median gross exposure is 10% and it holds two positions. Scaled up to match the index’s return it would carry roughly the index’s drawdown.

The binding constraint is frequency

Eighty-one signals in twenty-seven years — three a year across eighteen ETFs. Dropping the run-up filter doubles that to 167 and lifts the return to +3.8% at 3% risk, but the drawdown more than doubles too, to −40.8%. The filter is buying risk reduction, not return.

And the correlation problem the numbers understate: these eighteen ETFs fall together, because a broad 20% drawdown is what makes them all qualify at once. The two-position median hides that in 2008 and 2020 every slot held the same trade. A portfolio limit is not optional here, and this simulation does not impose one.

The whole market, and the price you pay

The ETF rule failed on frequency, so the fix was obvious: run it on every company instead of eighteen funds. That works — 964 crossings instead of 81, gross exposure of 19–57% instead of 3–10%. Then two gates go in front of it, quality because a company need not recover the way an index does, and price because a quality screen with no valuation cut just buys whatever has already been bid up.

Read straight down that column and the gates look like they are destroying value. They are not — they are shrinking the book. An arm passing 52 signals holds fewer positions than one passing 951 and earns less for that reason alone, which says nothing about whether it picked better names. So every arm is re-run with its risk budget solved to put all of them at the same 25% gross exposure, and each is set against the only null that matters: the same number of entries drawn at random from the drawdown pool.

The valuation gate fails, and fails backwards

Cheap quality names returned +5.0% at matched exposure — the 28th percentile of its own random null. Picking the cheap half did worse than picking at random from the same drawdowns. The expensive half returned +6.7%.

The premise was to avoid overpaying for quality. On this universe and this window, overpaying was the better half of the trade.

Quality is the only gate pointing the right way

+5.8% against a null median of +4.5%, at the 88th percentile. That is suggestive and it is not significant — one in eight random draws beat it. It is the same direction the three-year quality work found, which is the only reason to keep it.

And the eighteen percent itself does nothing here

The identical gates run at a 5% pullback return +4.9% at matched exposure against the 18% version’s +5.0%. On index funds the depth threshold was the entire finding. On single companies it is worth nothing, and the reason is not mysterious: an index is arithmetically obliged to recover if its constituents do, and a company is obliged to nothing. The tail says the same thing — the fifth-percentile further fall is −79% here against −60% for ETFs, and the first percentile is −97%, which is a company going to zero.

Two things that column of percentiles cannot carry. The 52-signal arm has a null band nine points wide, so its 72nd percentile is noise, not a result — the honest reading is that neither valuation direction separates from random, and cheap is the worse of the two. And matching exposure across arms of very different signal counts makes the sparse ones hugely concentrated: the expensive arm reaches 25% gross by holding about two positions at 12.5% of the book each, which is not a risk profile anyone should run even though its measured drawdown looks tame.

Nothing here beats owning the market

Equal-weight buy-and-hold of the same 134 names returned +18.1% a year over the window, 14.07× money. That figure is inflated — it is a present-day membership list, so every name in it survived to be listed, and DIA over the identical window returned +10.6%. Take the honest benchmark: the best arm at matched exposure is +6.7%, the best at any exposure is +8.3%. Every version loses.

The window matters too, and it cuts against the valuation result rather than for it. October 2010 to August 2026 is precisely the stretch in which expensive growth beat cheap value, so the failure of the cheap gate is at least partly a regime and not a law. It is one path, and it is the path we have.

When the recovery outruns the carry

Every test above measures how much a drawdown entry returns. That is the wrong quantity for a levered book. Carry accrues per unit of time and the recovery does not, so what decides whether borrowing pays is the rate, not the size. A +30% recovery over three years annualises to 9.1% and loses to a 5% borrow once the levered portion is paid for; the same +30% in six months annualises to 69%.

The answer is not the one the question expects

At a 5% borrow, carry is almost never the binding constraint. Of all 167 ETF drawdown entries, 80% recover by more than the carry they accrue. Conditioning on anything barely moves that: the best cell found here clears carry on 78% of entries against 80% for everything else. At realistic financing the question answers itself.

What actually kills a levered drawdown book is closeout, not carry. At 3×, 22% of positions in the best cell are wiped before the recovery arrives, and 31–33% on single stocks. That is the constraint worth engineering around.

Carry only starts to bind at borrow rates well above a broker’s. And that is where conditioning earns its keep — not by raising the chance of beating the carry, but by buying headroom on the rate you can afford to pay.

The mechanism is speed, and only speed. The fast-and-broad cell recovers in a median 179 sessions against 384 for everything else, on almost the same median gain. Half the time at risk means half the carry, which is the entire separation in that chart. It is also why the cell matters to a book even at a 5% borrow: capital that turns over twice as fast can be put to work twice as often.

It clears a proper null, and survives the extremes

+37.6% annualised against a quarter-block null median of +17.1%, band +10.3% to +29.6% — the 99th percentile. Dropping 2008–09 and 2020 entirely leaves it at +35.2% with a 127-session median hold. It is not a crisis artifact.

On single stocks it is marginal

+41.6% against a null band topping out at +42.0% — the 94th percentile, sitting on the edge. And the carry separation nearly vanishes: 56% vs 50% at a 30% borrow, against the ETF gap of 53% vs 18%. Same result as everywhere else on this page — the conditioner works on indices and thins out on individual companies.

What nearly fooled me here

The first null drew 64 entries at random and put the cell at the 100th percentile with a band half as wide. But those 64 entries are not 64 independent observations — they collapse into 17 episodes, and March 2020 alone supplies 16 of the entries. Drawing whole episodes instead widened the band from [+14.7%, +23.3%] to [+10.3%, +29.6%] and moved the result from certain to marginal.

The 2008 entries are the useful corrective: the two episodes spanning December 2007 and July 2008 returned −1.0% and −0.1% annualised. The deepest crisis in the sample is the one where fast-and-broad did not work.

One more artifact to name, because it inflates every number in this section’s right tail. Annualising a short hold explodes: a 25% gain in twenty sessions annualises past 800%, and the 95th percentile of single-stock entries reads +1,681%. Medians are used throughout for that reason, and the excess-over-carry column is the honest one because it never annualises at all. None of these rates are compounding returns — they are the breakeven borrow rate for one position over one holding period, and nothing guarantees the capital can be redeployed at anything like them.

The rule, stated

  1. Universe. Equity index and sector ETFs. Exclude futures-backed commodity and volatility products — they decay for reasons unrelated to drawdown and they were the entire source of an apparent cliff past 35% that turned out to be an artifact.
  2. Entry. 18% or more below the 252-day high — on index funds. On individual companies the threshold does no work and a 5% pullback performs identically, so there is no depth rule to state.
  3. Filter. Skip it if the peak it fell from had itself risen more than about a third in the preceding two years. That is a bubble deflating, and its tail is fifteen points worse.
  4. Exit. The prior high, or three years, whichever comes first. Never a trailing stop.
  5. Size. Risk budget divided by the fifth-percentile further fall: 60% for ETFs, so 1.7% of the book per position at 1% risk. For single companies that denominator is 79% and the position is smaller still, because the first percentile is −97% and that is a company going to zero.
  6. Price paid. No valuation gate. An earnings-yield cut against the universe median landed at the 28th percentile of its own null — worse than choosing at random from the same drawdowns. Keep the quality cut, which is weakly positive; drop the cheapness cut, which is not.
  7. Speed and breadth. The one conditioner that clears a block-resampled null: a fall of 80 sessions or less from the peak, taken while a quarter of the universe is already 10% down. It recovers in 179 sessions against 384, which is what lets it carry a 31% borrow where an ordinary entry breaks even near 16%. It holds on indices, thins to marginal on single stocks, and survives removing 2008–09 and 2020.
  8. Carry is not the binding constraint. At a 5% borrow, 80% of drawdown entries recover by more than they accrue. Engineer against closeout instead: 22% of 3× positions in the best cell are wiped before the recovery lands.
  9. Leverage. The measured ceiling is under 2× on a plain hold. A centred grid roughly halves the closeout rate, but about half of that benefit is simply that a centred grid deploys half its capital at entry.

Where this leaves the rule

Widening the universe fixed the thing it was supposed to fix. Exposure went from 3–10% to 19–57% and the frequency objection is gone. What the wider universe also did was remove the reason for the rule: the 18% threshold that carried the whole ETF result is worth nothing on individual companies, where a 5% pullback with the same gates returns the same. The depth mattered because an index must recover if its constituents do. Nothing obliges a company to.

So there are two findings and only one of them survives contact with a null. Quality selects, weakly, at the 88th percentile. Valuation selects backwards. And every arm loses to owning the market outright.

The one honest measurement gap left is intraday. Fills here are taken off the close, so any grid harvest is a floor rather than an estimate, and a tight grid genuinely cannot be evaluated on daily bars.

Two universes. 18 US equity index and sector ETFs on split-adjusted daily bars, 1999–2026; and 134 US companies with both price history and readable SEC facts, October 2010 to August 2026, the window being set by the first month the fundamentals panel is dense enough to take a cross-sectional median. That company list is present-day membership, so it is survivorship-biased upward, and the equal-weight benchmark it is measured against carries the identical bias — DIA over the same window returned +10.6% against the list’s +18.1%, which is roughly the size of the distortion. Company facts come from the SEC store, whose ten concepts contain no free cash flow, EBITDA, net debt or interest coverage, so the valuation gate is an earnings yield against the cross-section and emphatically not a discount to appraised fair value; revenue is read from both the pre- and post-ASC-606 tags, since most companies switched in 2018 and never backfilled. Facts are unusable until 90 days after their period closes because the store carries no filing date. Arms are compared at matched gross exposure against 25 random draws of equal size from the same drawdown pool. Futures-backed commodity funds, volatility products, currencies, bonds and physical metals are excluded: the first two decay independently of any drawdown, and the rest have no earnings against which a discount could be measured. Drawdown is measured against the trailing 252-session high; entries are fresh crossings, re-armed only after recovering to within 60% of the threshold. The further fall is the deepest point below entry over the following three years, taken from daily lows so an intraday spike that would trigger a margin call is counted. Portfolio returns charge 5% a year on any borrowed portion; episode returns do not, and every grid figure quoted elsewhere in this programme is gross of financing, which at 3× over three years is worth more than the entire harvest. Fills are taken off the close, never off the high and low of the same bar — crediting both would assume the price fell and then rose on every single day, which is worth several hundred percent of imaginary return over three years. Three-year windows over twenty-seven years give roughly nine independent periods, and the deepest drawdown buckets are dominated by 2008–09 and March 2020.
