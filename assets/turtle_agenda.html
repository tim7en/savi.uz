After the Ninth Rejection

Eleven proposals tested and rejected, one real improvement banked — and a much narrower view of what that improvement actually is. As of this revision the narrowing has gone further: the edge that looked like it belonged to the 30-minute interval turns out to belong mostly to an optimistic cost assumption. This is what is worth testing next, ranked by evidence, with the kill criterion for each fixed before it runs.

What changed in this revision

Three results since 19 August, two of which narrow the banked case rather than extend it.

  1. T2.3 CFTC positioning is closed. Zero of eighteen pre-registered arms beat their own circular-shift null. The binding constraint was never the data: positioning is so autocorrelated that 991 weekly filings carry 15 to 45 independent observations. Repairing the release dates first was essential — the old as-of keying leaked 97% of the following week's move. Full write-up in The Eleventh Rejection.
  2. T1.9 has answered its own kill criterion early, and the answer is bad. On the twelve months of 30-minute data held so far, the non-equity book scores Sharpe 2.57 at 2bp and 0.15 at the 15bp gate. Thirteen of the twenty-one names take zero trades at that cost. Worse, the untradeable thirteen are the duration and FX names the whole diversification case rested on.
  3. A standing control is added: effective sample size, reported beside every nominal count, computed before a test rather than after it. This is the second proposal to fail on episode count after looking well-powered by row count.

What the strategy actually is

The most important finding in the programme, and it arrived last. Read this before anything else on the page.

The banked configuration was described throughout as a Turtle system — a trend follower. Running the identical rules on daily bars across asset classes, which is the actual Turtle setup, settles whether that description was earned.

Two independent factors, each worth roughly half the distance. The bar interval alone costs −1.35 Sharpe — same names, same decade, same rules, only 30-minute becoming daily. Universe and period cost a further −1.16, ending at essentially nothing across twenty years with ten negative years out of twenty. The daily cross-asset row has now been reproduced from source rather than quoted: 1,491 trades, Sharpe 0.13, ten negative years out of twenty, worst year −1.15.

The interval advantage is mostly a cost artifact. The last two rows are the same 21 instruments over the same twelve months, differing only in what a round trip is assumed to cost. At the 2bp the programme has been using, the 30-minute book scores 2.57. At the 15bp its own kill criterion names, it scores 0.15 — statistically indistinguishable from the daily book's 0.13.

This does not overturn the equity result, which has not yet been re-swept at 15bp on the current universe. It does mean the decomposition above should be read with a third factor beside interval and universe: how much of the 30-minute edge is priced away by trading it. That question now outranks everything except T1.1.

Caveat carried in both directions: the two 30-minute rows cover one benign year for commodities, not the twenty that the daily row covers. A same-period daily comparison is not possible — 251 sessions falls below the engine's 400-bar minimum — so period and interval remain partly confounded here.

This is an intraday US-equity breakout system, not a trend-following system. It uses Turtle-shaped rules, but the original Turtle setup — daily bars, many asset classes — produces 0.13 here. That is a narrower and more fragile object than the framing this programme had been using, and it retroactively explains a great deal: why daily-frequency literature kept failing to apply, why macro regime signals (a weekly phenomenon) never bit, why nine overlays borrowed from the managed-futures world all died.

It does not mean the result is false. Intraday momentum is a documented effect in its own right. It does mean the edge should be described as what it is, sized with that fragility in mind, and validated out-of-sample before it is traded — which is T1.1, and which just became the most urgent item here.

Where we are

Pure price. Every options pathway tested and closed; every macro pathway tested and closed; positioning tested and closed.

Entry is a 55-bar Donchian breakout, long-only, stop-filled, on 30-minute bars. Sizing is risk / N on Wilder ATR(20), hard stop 2N, pyramid ½N to four units, six concurrent positions. The exit is a fixed 3N chandelier — the one change that paid, worth Sharpe 2.63 against 2.05 for the Donchian exit it replaced, winning in ten years out of ten, and it unlocked breadth the old exit had been capping.

Selection and survivorship, measured. Nine of the 42 names had not listed in 2017 — UBER, CRWD, HIMS, ASTS, COIN, HOOD, RKLB, RIVN, GEV — and could not have been held. Dropping them costs 13pp of CAGR. Restricting to the 13 ETFs, which carry almost no single-name selection bias, takes Sharpe from 2.63 to 2.14.

But most of that gap is breadth, not hindsight: the breadth curve predicts 47.1% CAGR at 15 names and the 13 ETFs deliver 47.5%, dead on their size, while the 29 single names deliver 49.3% where their size predicts about 64.6%. The low-bias part of the universe is pulling more than its weight. Honest estimate of the selection premium: −0.1 to −0.5 Sharpe, not the −34pp the headline suggests.

The non-equity sleeve genuinely diversifies. Splitting the ETF book in two: seven equity ETFs score Sharpe 1.62, six non-equity ones (GLD, SLV, XLE, TMF, TBT, UVXY) score 1.31, and together they score 2.09 — better than either alone. Their daily R streams correlate +0.031, and +0.004 on the equity sleeve's down days. Zero, and zero exactly where it matters.

Six uncorrelated instruments bought as much Sharpe (+0.47) as thirty-two additional equities did (+0.50). Roughly a five-fold efficiency ratio, and the strongest remaining argument for expanding outside equities. The limit: in 2022 both sleeves were negative, so zero average correlation does not guarantee diversification in any particular bad year.

Options closed on the ceiling test — an instrument's own realised volatility, the quantity any chain would estimate, underperforms a fixed trail, so the constraint is redundancy with N rather than forecast accuracy. Macro closed at p = 0.453, beaten by its own reversal, on a sample containing exactly one yield-curve inversion which happens to be the historical exception.

The agenda

Tier 1 protects what is banked and closes the gaps that need no new data. Nothing in Tier 2 starts until Tier 1 is done.

TIER 1

T1.1

Validate the exit out-of-sample

Now urgent~hours

Chandelier 3N was chosen as the best of twelve variants, scored on the same 42 names it is now credited with. The fragility decomposition raises the stakes: a result that halves when the bar interval changes is a result whose robustness cannot be assumed anywhere else either.

Method
Walk-forward on two axes — choose on a subset of names, score on held-out names, rotating; and choose on 2017–2021, score on 2022–2026.
Control
Report the selection-set advantage beside the held-out advantage. Shrinkage is expected; collapse is disqualifying.
Kill
If the held-out advantage over Donchian is under half the in-sample advantage, treat 3N as fitted and fall back to the median of the top three rather than the argmax.
T1.9

Intraday history for the non-equity ETFs

Mostly answereddownload running

The premise was that six uncorrelated instruments are worth thirty-two correlated ones, and that the edge lives at 30 minutes and dies at daily. Together they named one acquisition: intraday bars for the non-equity ETFs — USO, UNG, DBC, DBA, TLT, IEF, UUP, FXE, FXY, VXX, HYG, LQD, GDX.

Twelve months of all 21 are now held, and the full 2017–2026 backfill is running. The twelve months are already enough to answer the kill criterion, because it is a question about volatility against cost rather than about sample length.

The acquisition's premise is inverted. At a 15bp round trip, thirteen of the twenty-one names take zero trades — not losing trades, none at all. The engine's N floor is five times the round trip, so a name needs roughly 0.75% of price in volatility before a position can be sized, and the median 30-minute bar range for the duration and FX names is an order of magnitude below that: SHY and FXB at 0.023%, TIP 0.049%, FXE and FXY 0.056%, HYG and IEF 0.062%, LQD 0.078%, TLT 0.133%.

Only four names hold positive net R at 15bp — USO, BNO, PPLT and GSG, the last on two trades. The names that clear the cost floor are energy and metals, correlated with each other; the duration and FX names that carried the diversification argument are precisely the ones that cannot trade. The correlation case and the tradeability case point at disjoint sets of instruments.

Result
Whole book: Sharpe 2.57 and 741 trades at 2bp, 0.15 and 209 trades at 15bp, negative by 20bp. A far steeper decline than the “about Sharpe 2.3 at 15bp” this item anticipated for the equity book.
Open
Whether the floor is regime-dependent. Twelve calm months understate bond volatility badly — TLT's 30-minute range in 2022 was several times its current 0.133% — so the backfill is still worth finishing before duration is written off at this interval.
Kill
Unchanged and now largely applied: any name that does not clear the cost sweep at 15bp is dropped. Uncorrelated and untradeable is worth nothing.
T1.7

Extend the sample to 2000

Data now in hand~day

A precondition for asking the macro question at all. index_prices holds daily S&P 500, Nasdaq 100 and Russell 2000 back to January 2000, fed_path and the GSW curve reach equally far, and the 21 non-equity ETFs now add daily history from 2002–2010.

For yield-curve inversion this is the whole ballgame: the current sample holds one episode and it is a false positive, while 2000–2026 adds the July 2000 and February 2006 inversions, which preceded declines of 22% and 34%. One false positive becomes two true and one false. It also brings 2008 into view, the only genuine bear market anywhere in this programme's data.

Method
Run the validated configuration on daily bars, 2000–2026. Confirm it reproduces known behaviour on the overlapping window first.
Caveat
Daily rules score 1.29 on equities and 0.13 cross-asset, so this book is evidence about regimes, never a performance estimate. Do not let a daily backtest become the thing being optimised.
T1.3

Correlation-aware capacity

~hours

The book caps at six concurrent positions and, when more signals compete than slots, picks among them at random. Six semiconductor names is not six positions. With the non-equity sleeve about to grow, choosing slots by correlation rather than coin-flip becomes materially more valuable than it was.

Method
Replace the random tie-break with a correlation-penalised choice, or cap exposure per cluster.
Control
Matched drawdown — this reduces exposure, and that is the confound that made theme sub-accounts look bad before it was corrected.
Kill
Must beat the random tie-break at matched drawdown in ≥7 of 10 years.
T1.6

Equity-curve de-risking

~hours

Cut gross exposure while the equity curve is in drawdown, restore on recovery. No second book, no new data, and it conditions on realised portfolio state rather than a forecast — which puts it outside the family that has failed ten times.

Method
Halve risk below X% from peak, restore above. Sweep X widely and report the whole surface, not the best cell.
Control
Matched drawdown, plus a shuffled-trigger null at matching frequency. The rule needs serial dependence in the equity curve; a random schedule must not reproduce the gain.
Kill
Must beat a permanently lower risk level at matched drawdown in ≥7 of 10 years. Most published equity-curve rules fail exactly this.
T1.5

Size the hedge as insurance, not alpha

~day · better after T1.7

The short side is the hedge, and it is measured: standalone Sharpe 1.00, but it lifted 2022 from 0.14 to 1.87 while costing roughly 12 percentage points of CAGR in aggregate. The temptation is to optimise the short weight on this sample. Do not — the effective sample for a hedging decision is the number of drawdowns, and there are two.

Method
Fixed short weightings of 0%, 15%, 30%, 50% of risk budget. Report premium paid in bull years against drawdown relief in 2018 and 2022 separately, never blended.
Control
Against a smaller long book and against holding cash, at matched drawdown. A hedge must beat simply carrying less risk.
Kill
If a hedged book never beats an equally de-risked unhedged book, the short sleeve is a costlier way to do nothing.
T1.2

Earnings-aware position management

~days · needs calendar

Do not predict the surprise on the first pass — that is a direction problem, and direction has never worked here. Ask the risk question: earnings are a dated, known-in-advance gap risk, which puts them in the management layer where every payoff in this programme has come from.

Groundwork
equity.db already carries an analyst_earnings table with exactly the right shape — ticker, reported date, reported and estimated EPS, surprise — and zero rows. Schema done; only population remains.
Method
Four arms: flat into the announcement, half size through it, wider stop through it, do nothing.
Data trap
Use announcement dates as known ex-ante. Many vendors silently restate dates and backfill estimates — audit before trusting.
Kill
Beats do-nothing at matched drawdown in ≥7 of 10 years.

T2.4

Long/short macro regime tilt

gated on T1.7

Run once on the current sample and it failed outright — Sharpe 2.24 against 2.63 banked, four years of ten, p = 0.453, and beaten by its own reversal at 2.35. That is less evidence against the hypothesis than confirmation that one inversion episode cannot test one.

A refinement to pre-register now, from the historical record: recessions frequently begin after the inversion ends — ten months in 1989, six in 2006, three in 2000. The tradeable signal, if any, may be the un-inversion rather than the inverted state. Fix this before the extended sample exists and the temptation to fit appears.

Method
Weight long against short by regime label, lagged one session, from market-priced rates only. Sweep the tilt and report the surface.
Power
Report episode count, not session count. Fifteen episodes justifies a tilt at most — never a hard switch.
Kill
Must clear the persistence-matched null, and exceed the calendar split, and beat its own reversal. Failing any, macro is dropped as a trading input.
T2.3

CFTC positioning

Closed — rejecteddone

The first candidate that was not a volatility forecast, and it failed anyway. Eighteen pre-registered arms — three contracts, three trader categories, one- and four-week horizons — and none beat its own circular-shift null. Reported in full in The Eleventh Rejection.

Both defects were real, and both were mis-sized here. The mixed date formats were overstated: report_date is clean ISO in every table and agrees with the US-format column on all 18,911 rows where both exist. The corruption sits in a redundant column that nothing needs to read. The missing release date was understated: keying on the as-of Tuesday leaked 97% of the following week's average absolute move, not three days of hindsight. A release calendar now derives publication from observed NYSE sessions.

Why it failed
Not the signal, the sample. Net positioning has lag-one autocorrelation between 0.91 and 0.97, so 991 weekly filings carry 15 to 45 independent observations — about 3% of the row count. The test could only ever have detected an enormous effect.
Near miss
E-mini S&P asset-manager positioning at four weeks: spread −1.49% at p = 0.055, the contrarian direction. Rejected anyway — eighteen arms make one such result expected by chance, its effective sample is 30, and the same arm at one week gives p = 0.133.
Verdict
Dropped permanently, on effective sample size rather than on data quality. No vendor or history length fixes that. The release calendar stays: it is now the only correct way to join these tables for any purpose, including reporting.

The standing controls contract

Applies to every item above without exception. Every rule was written after a specific mistake.

  1. Matched drawdown, always. Any change that reduces risk must be compared at equal exposure. Cutting risk mechanically improves a losing strategy, and that has produced a false positive here more than once.
  2. A rival forecast, not just a null. The question is never “better than nothing” but “better than the cheap price-based alternative” — which is what killed the gamma trail.
  3. Persistence-matched nulls. Any autocorrelated state variable gets a circular-shift control preserving its persistence while destroying its alignment to price.
  4. Beat your own reversal. A signal performing no better than its opposite carries no information, whatever its p-value says. Nearly free, and it caught the macro tilt.
  5. Validate every derived series against known history before using it. A regime label that does not reproduce events you can look up is not measuring what its name says. Confusing GSW forward rates for zero-coupon yields silently deleted the largest inversion on record; the error was invisible in the code and obvious the moment the episode dates were printed.
  6. The kill criterion is written before the run. Not after seeing the result.
  7. Per-year consistency, not aggregate significance. ≥7 of 10 years. An aggregate p-value hides a result that lived in one regime.
  8. Mark-to-market accounting. Open positions hit the equity curve on the day they move, not the day they close. Exit-marked drawdowns understate risk by different amounts for different exit rules.
  9. Trailing percentiles and a one-session lag. Never full-sample boundaries, never same-session features. Both have leaked into this work and had to be found and fixed.
  10. Change one thing at a time. The cross-asset daily run moved interval, universe, asset class and period at once and was uninterpretable until the interval was isolated separately.
  11. Report the reversal. Where a result was withdrawn or a prediction proved wrong, it is recorded rather than quietly dropped. Three claims have been withdrawn so far.
  12. Effective sample size, computed before the test. Every autocorrelated series gets n × (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) reported beside its row count, and the test is judged against that. CFTC positioning looked like 991 observations and was 30; the macro tilt looked like a decade of sessions and was one inversion episode. Two proposals have now died of this after appearing well-powered.
  13. Quote costs at the level you would actually pay. Headline numbers here assume a 2bp round trip. The 30-minute cross-asset book scores 2.57 there and 0.15 at 15bp, so a cost assumption can be worth more than an interval choice. Every result gets a cost sweep, not a single point.

What is still open

The options download. Running, four symbols complete, aggregates only. Its sole remaining use is T2.1 — direction, not volatility. Nothing in Tier 1 waits on it, and the disk sits at 95%.

The bull-sample problem. Eight of the ten years in the intraday book were bull years. That inflates every level quoted here and makes T1.5 a judgement call rather than a measurement. T1.7 helps for regime questions but cannot fix it for the traded book — only a longer intraday history or live results will.

The fragility itself. Nothing on this list explains why 30-minute breakouts work where daily ones do not. Until that is understood, the honest position is that the edge is real in-sample and of unknown durability out of it.

Whether the interval advantage survives its own costs. Newly open, and the most consequential question here. On the cross-asset book the 30-minute edge over daily almost entirely disappears at 15bp. The banked equity book has not been re-swept at that level on the current universe, and until it has, the headline 2.64 should be read as a 2bp number rather than an expectation.
