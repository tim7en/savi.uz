The Eleventh Rejection

Twenty years of weekly positioning data, eighteen pre-registered arms, and not one that beats its own shuffled copy. The interesting part is not that it failed — it is that a thousand observations turned out to be thirty.

The finding

CFTC positioning does not predict index returns at one or four weeks, in any trader category, on any of the three contracts tested. Every quintile spread sits inside the 5–95% band of its own circular-shift null. The best arm reaches p = 0.055; across eighteen arms, chance alone was expected to produce about one result that good.

The deeper result is about power, and it generalises past this test. Positioning is so persistent week to week that 991 weekly observations carry roughly 30 independent ones. This study was never capable of detecting anything but an enormous effect, and no amount of additional history fixes that at the rate the effective sample grows.

The data had to be repaired before it could be tested

Two defects, both load-bearing. One was smaller than recorded, the other much larger.

Mixed date formats — overstated

The agenda recorded that stored dates mix ISO and US M/D/YYYY formats, so ordering was wrong. Ordering was in fact fine: report_date is clean ISO in every table and agrees with the US-format column on all 18,911 rows where both exist, with zero mismatches.

The corruption is confined to the redundant report_date_as_yyyy_mm_dd column, which in the legacy table contains its own header string in all 251,506 rows. Nothing needed re-downloading; the column simply is not used.

Missing release date — understated

The report is compiled as of Tuesday and published the following Friday, but only the Tuesday was ever stored. Keying a study on it grants hindsight over four of the five sessions in the weekly cycle — measured against the S&P over 1,381 weeks, 97% of the average absolute weekly move.

A release calendar now derives publication from observed NYSE sessions, so holiday weeks slip correctly to Monday. Every result here is keyed on the first session after publication, with the as-of arm run alongside purely to measure the bias.

The 2018–19 shutdown gets no release date at all. The CFTC kept compiling through the lapse and backfilled the backlog on resumption, so the as-of series in this database is continuous and the delay is invisible from the data. Those five weeks are flagged shutdown_unknown and dropped rather than assigned an invented catch-up schedule — which is what a naive plus-three-days rule would have done, across precisely the window where a positioning signal looks best.

A thousand rows, thirty observations

Positioning changes slowly. Adjusting the sample size for that persistence is the difference between a test that looked well-powered and one that never was.

Why the count collapses

Net positioning has a lag-one autocorrelation between 0.91 and 0.97 depending on the series. At 0.94, consecutive weeks are almost the same observation recorded twice, and the independent count falls to n × (1−ρ)/(1+ρ) — about 3% of what the row count advertises.

Effective sample ranged from 15 to 45 across the nine release-keyed arms. Dealer positioning was worst at 15, because it is the most persistent of the three categories.

What that costs

This is the same failure the programme met at proposal T2.4, where a yield-curve tilt was tested on a sample containing one inversion episode. Both look like large samples by row count and are tiny by episode count.

Extending the history helps far less than it appears to. Independent observations accumulate at roughly one per eight months here, so reaching even 100 would take another forty years of filings.

The near miss, and why it is not one

One arm came close enough to be worth naming explicitly, because leaving it unnamed is how a null result quietly becomes a positive one later.

E-mini S&P 500 asset-manager positioning at four weeks produced a spread of −1.49% at p = 0.055 — high asset-manager crowding preceding lower returns, which is the contrarian direction the folk claim predicts. The sign is right and the magnitude is not trivial.

It still fails, for three reasons that were all fixed in advance. Eighteen arms were tested, so roughly one result at p ≤ 0.056 was expected from noise; a multiplicity-adjusted threshold would be near 0.003. Its effective sample is 30, so the quintiles hold about six independent observations each. And the same arm at one week gives p = 0.133 with a spread an order of magnitude smaller — a real effect at four weeks should leave some trace at one.

Disposition

The kill criterion was fixed before the run: an arm survives only if it clears the circular-shift null at p < 0.05 and keeps its sign under the release-date lag. Nothing cleared, so positioning closes.

  • Positioning is dropped as a trading input. Not deferred pending better data — the binding constraint is the effective sample size, and that is a property of the series, not of the vendor or the history length.
  • The release calendar stays. It is now the only correct way to join these tables, and any future use of COT data — including as a reporting or regime-description input — must key on it.
  • Any prior positioning result is void, not adjusted. Work keyed on the as-of date captured 97% of the following week's move as hindsight. That is not a bias to correct for; it invalidates the measurement.
  • Report effective sample size, not row count, everywhere. This is the second proposal in the programme to fail on episode count after looking well-powered by observation count. It should be a standing column in every future screen, computed before the test rather than after it.

What would change the answer: a positioning measure that is not autocorrelated — the weekly change in net position, or a surprise against a published expectation, rather than the level. That is a different hypothesis with a different null, and it is not what T2.3 proposed. If it is ever run, it goes through the contract as its own pre-registered test.
